Behind the atlas

History belongs
to all of us.

Black Atlas brings African American history back to the places where people lived it.

01 About the project

A place to follow the connections between a street, a city, a movement, and the people who made it matter.

This atlas is still taking shape. The photographs have individual credits; city pages bring together sourced introductions, and dated map layers follow the selected year.

02 How to explore

Choose a chapter to change the photographs and the map together. Move the timeline to a particular year, or select a place on the map.

Drag to turn the map. Scroll or pinch to zoom. Right-drag to pan. The reset button brings you home.

The timeline starts in 1619. African presence in the Americas reaches further back; this is a starting point for this atlas, not the beginning of that history.

03 About the author

A personal introduction from the creator will be added here as the project develops.

04 Sources & method

Historical jurisdictions use the Newberry Library’s Atlas of Historical County Boundaries (1783–2000). Later years use modern Census boundaries. The terrain and coastline remain a modern geographic reference, with Alaska and Hawaiʻi insets.

Boundaries are sampled on December 31. Civil War colors show state affiliations, including border states that remained in the Union. They do not show military occupation. Every photograph has its own date and credit.

    About this prototype

    City markers follow the inclusive date ranges of published stories, not city lifespans. Historical jurisdiction boundaries cover 1783–2000; earlier years show geographic context without state lines. Later years use modern Census boundaries. This layer records U.S. jurisdictions and selected claims, not the full geography of Indigenous nations or sovereignty.

Relief has been downsampled and visually exaggerated, and the land colouring is an illustrative palette drawn from elevation, slope and position — not a land-cover survey.
